How to read candlestick charts

Candlesticks pack four prices into one simple shape. Once you can read one candle, you can read the whole chart.

Published 11 November 2026 · RealChart

The candlestick is the most popular way to show price on a chart, and for good reason: each candle tells you, at a glance, where price opened, closed, and how far it travelled in a given period. Here's how to read them.

What one candle shows

Each candle covers a single slice of time — one minute, one hour, one day, whatever timeframe you've chosen. It has two parts:

  • The body — the thick block — spans the open and close prices for that period.
  • The wicks (or shadows) — the thin lines above and below — reach up to the high and down to the low.

Colour tells you the direction. A green (or hollow) candle closed higher than it opened — buyers were in control. A red (or filled) candle closed lower than it opened — sellers were in control.

Reading the shape

The proportions matter as much as the colour:

  • A long body means strong, one-sided movement. A short body means a quiet, balanced period.
  • A long wick shows price pushed in that direction but was rejected — it couldn't hold. Long wicks often mark turning points.
  • Little or no wick means price closed near its extreme, a sign of conviction.

Five patterns worth knowing

  • Doji — open and close almost equal, so the body is a thin line. Signals indecision; often a pause before a move.
  • Hammer — a small body with a long lower wick, after a fall. Buyers rejected lower prices; a potential bottom.
  • Shooting star — a small body with a long upper wick, after a rise. Sellers rejected higher prices; a potential top.
  • Bullish engulfing — a big green candle that fully covers the previous red one. A shift toward buyers.
  • Bearish engulfing — a big red candle that swallows the previous green one. A shift toward sellers.

Put it into practice

Patterns are hints, not guarantees — they work best with context, such as a support level or a trend. The fastest way to learn is to open a chart, switch it to candlesticks, and scroll back through real price history looking for these shapes.
